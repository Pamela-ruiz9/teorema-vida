## Abstract

Generational labels such as *Baby Boomers*, *Generation X*, *Millennials*, and *Generation Z* have become dominant tools for describing cohort differences in values, behavior, and life meaning. However, these labels were constructed primarily from United States-specific historical events—post-WWII prosperity, the GI Bill, Vietnam, Watergate, the Reagan era, 9/11, the 2008 financial crisis, and COVID-19—and are frequently exported uncritically to contexts where their explanatory power is limited. This paper proposes a multidimensional vector model that represents life meaning as an eight-dimensional vector **S = (Security, Status, Autonomy, Affiliation, Experience, Identity, Transcendence, Resilience)**, computed from five regime variables: Economic scarcity (E), Technological disruption (T), Institutional trust (C), Structural violence (V), and Informational saturation (I). The model is parameterized over the Mannheimian formative window (ages 10–25) and applied to the four canonical U.S. generations. Results yield distinct vector signatures whose magnitudes range from 14.6 to 16.2 and whose inter-generational distances reveal that change in U.S. life meaning has been driven less by mere temporal succession than by structural shifts in trust and technology. A brief cross-national contrast with Mexico shows that Millennials and Generation Z exhibit notable international convergence on identity and experience, while resilience remains highly country-specific.

**Keywords:** generational theory, life meaning, vector model, cohort analysis, formative window, United States, sociology of knowledge

## I. Introduction

The vocabulary of generations has become one of the most pervasive lenses through which contemporary societies interpret cultural and economic change. Media, marketing, policy, and increasingly academic research rely on labels such as *Baby Boomers*, *Generation X*, *Millennials*, and *Generation Z* to explain phenomena ranging from labor-market participation to political polarization and mental health outcomes [1], [4], [11]. Yet these labels are neither neutral nor universal. They were constructed, chiefly by U.S. historians and demographers, around events specific to the United States: the post-World War II economic boom, the GI Bill of 1944, the Vietnam War, the Watergate scandal, the Reagan-era neoliberal turn, the September 11 attacks, the 2008 financial crisis, and the COVID-19 pandemic.

When such labels are imported into other national contexts, they tend to produce two analytical distortions. First, they collapse heterogeneous regime conditions—economic scarcity, institutional trust, technological exposure, violence, information saturation—into a single temporal axis of birth year. Second, they suppress the possibility that two individuals born in the same year but formed under radically different structural conditions may develop divergent value priorities. The result is a taxonomy that describes cohorts without explaining them.

This paper proposes an alternative. It develops a multidimensional *vector model of life meaning* in which each cohort—national or otherwise—is represented as a vector **S** in an eight-dimensional space of life priorities, computed deterministically from five regime variables that describe the conditions prevailing during the cohort's *formative window*, defined following Mannheim as ages 10–25 [1]. The model is then applied to the four canonical U.S. generations to demonstrate three claims: (i) the United States itself is a specific case rather than a universal template; (ii) the observed drift across Boomers, Gen X, Millennials, and Gen Z is dominated by changes in institutional trust (C) and technology (T) rather than by economic scarcity (E) alone; and (iii) the model produces interpretable inter-generational distances that align with well-documented cultural narratives.

Three considerations motivate the specific eight-dimensional structure adopted here. First, the dimensions are chosen to span the principal families of value identified in the post-Maslow literature: material security, social standing, self-direction, belonging, hedonic experience, expressive selfhood, meaning beyond the self, and adaptive capacity [2], [3], [13]. Second, the dimensions are distinguishable empirically: each corresponds to item clusters repeatedly documented in the World Values Survey [10] and in psychometric work on life orientation [14]. Third, the dimensions are deliberately non-orthogonal at the conceptual level—identity and experience, for instance, are empirically correlated—but the model represents them as separate axes so that regime-driven shifts along each axis become visible rather than hidden.

A second motivating contrast concerns the implicit causal model behind narrative labels. Journalistic and marketing treatments of generations typically treat cohort labels as exogenous causes of behavior: Millennials allegedly do X *because* they are Millennials. The vector model reverses this logic. Birth cohort is an indexing device; the causal work is done by the regime variables (E, T, C, V, I) prevailing during formation. A cohort vector is therefore a *prediction* from regime inputs, not a stipulation.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section II reviews the principal generational theories and their limits. Section III formalizes the vector model. Section IV applies it to the United States and contrasts the results with a Mexican baseline. Section V outlines preliminary validation through a web-based self-assessment instrument. Section VI discusses implications and limitations, and Section VII concludes.

## II. Related Work

### A. The Sociology of Generations

The modern sociological treatment of generations begins with Karl Mannheim's 1928 essay *The Problem of Generations* [1], which distinguishes biological birth cohorts from *generations as social units*—groups bound by shared exposure to historical events during a psychologically formative period. Mannheim located this formative window between adolescence and early adulthood, a range subsequently refined in developmental psychology to approximately ages 10–25. Mannheim's central insight—that it is not birth year itself but the historical conditions of formation that generate a generation—is the foundation of the model presented here.

### B. Needs, Values, and Post-Materialism

Parallel to Mannheim's structural account, two value-centered traditions emerged. Maslow's hierarchy of needs [2] proposed a stratification of human motivation from physiological security to self-actualization. Inglehart's post-materialist thesis [3] extended this logic to the cohort level, arguing that individuals formed under conditions of economic security develop value priorities distinct from those formed under scarcity. The World Values Survey [10] has documented this cross-national pattern for five decades. The present model can be read as a formalization and extension of the Maslow–Inglehart intuition, replacing a one-dimensional material/post-material axis with an eight-dimensional space.

### C. American Generational Theory

Strauss and Howe [4] popularized a cyclical account of American generations in which four archetypal types recur in a fixed sequence. Their framework has been widely criticized for historicism and for its tendency to universalize American experience. Twenge's empirical work on *iGen* [11] and Putnam's analysis of social capital decline in *Bowling Alone* [12] offer more measured contributions, documenting specific shifts in psychological well-being and civic engagement. However, each remains largely descriptive: they catalogue differences without providing a generative mechanism that links structural conditions to value outcomes.

### D. Regime-Level Comparative Work

A fourth strand of relevant literature comes from comparative historical sociology and from regime-level political economy. Inglehart and Welzel [13] demonstrate that value change proceeds along two orthogonal dimensions—traditional/secular and survival/self-expression—and that national trajectories along these dimensions track economic development and institutional modernization. Erikson's work on identity formation [14] further supports the idea that adolescence and early adulthood are the critical period during which individuals consolidate a coherent life narrative, reinforcing the formative-window assumption of Section III-E. The present model synthesizes these strands: a Mannheim-style formative window indexes *when* values form; an Inglehart–Welzel logic describes *how* regime conditions determine their content; and an Erikson-style developmental account justifies the centrality of ages 10–25.

### E. Gaps Addressed

The vector model proposed here differs from prior work on three axes. First, it is *generative*: vectors are computed from regime variables rather than inferred from survey aggregates. This allows prediction for cohorts or national contexts for which direct survey data is thin or absent. Second, it is *portable*: the same equations apply to any country or sub-national cohort, enabling genuine comparison across regimes. Third, it is *geometric*: differences between cohorts are expressed as Euclidean distances in a common space, rather than as narrative labels whose comparability is difficult to establish.

## III. The Vector Model

### A. Definition

Let a cohort be characterized by an eight-dimensional *life-meaning vector*

$$\mathbf{S} = (S_1, S_2, S_3, S_4, S_5, S_6, S_7, S_8)$$

whose components represent the relative prominence, on a 0–10 scale, of eight normative priorities:

1. **S₁ Security** — physical, economic, and ontological stability.
2. **S₂ Status** — social recognition, achievement, upward mobility.
3. **S₃ Autonomy** — self-direction, independence from institutional mediation.
4. **S₄ Affiliation** — belonging, community, interpersonal trust.
5. **S₅ Experience** — novelty-seeking, sensorial richness, lived moments.
6. **S₆ Identity** — self-construction, narrative coherence, expressive selfhood.
7. **S₇ Transcendence** — meaning beyond the self; spirituality or purpose.
8. **S₈ Resilience** — adaptive capacity under adversity.

### B. Regime Variables

Each vector is generated from five regime variables that summarize the structural conditions prevailing during the cohort's formative window:

- **E**: Economic scarcity (0 = abundance, 10 = severe scarcity).
- **T**: Technological disruption (0 = stable, 10 = maximally disruptive).
- **C**: Institutional trust (0 = collapsed, 10 = high).
- **V**: Structural violence (0 = peace, 10 = pervasive).
- **I**: Informational saturation (derived from T; 0 = scarce information, 10 = saturation).

Institutional trust is operationalized using representative public-trust surveys such as those produced by the Pew Research Center [5]. Structural violence is estimated from homicide and related crime rates [6]. Economic scarcity combines unemployment, real-wage growth, and household debt indicators [7]. Technological disruption and informational saturation are indexed by internet penetration and media consumption data [8].

### C. Generative Equations

Each dimension S<sub>i</sub> is a linear combination of regime variables passed through a clipping function f(x) = min(10, max(0, round(x, 1))):

$$S_1 = f(9 - 0.4E + 0.3C - 0.15V)$$

$$S_2 = f(7 - 0.35E + 0.2C - 0.3T)$$

$$S_3 = f(1 + 0.35E + 0.25T - 0.25C + 0.1V)$$

$$S_4 = f(4 + 0.35C - 0.25V - 0.05T)$$

$$S_5 = f(1 + 0.45T + 0.15I)$$

$$S_6 = f(0.5 + 0.35T + 0.35I - 0.15C)$$

$$S_7 = f(3 - 0.2E - 0.2V + 0.15C + 0.2T + 0.1I)$$

$$S_8 = f(0.5 + 0.45E + 0.35V - 0.15C)$$

The signs encode theoretical priors consistent with the literature: economic scarcity suppresses security and status while elevating autonomy and resilience; institutional trust raises security, status, and affiliation while dampening autonomy and identity; technology and information saturate experience and identity; violence erodes affiliation while reinforcing resilience and autonomy. The coefficients were calibrated against qualitative descriptions of well-documented cohorts and are subject to empirical refinement.

### D. Magnitude and Distance

The magnitude of a cohort vector is given by

$$|\mathbf{S}| = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{8} S_i^2}$$

and the distance between two cohort vectors **S**<sup>(a)</sup> and **S**<sup>(b)</sup> by the Euclidean norm

$$d(\mathbf{S}^{(a)}, \mathbf{S}^{(b)}) = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{8} (S_i^{(a)} - S_i^{(b)})^2}.$$

Magnitude captures the *intensity* of life-meaning demands, while distance captures the *qualitative shift* between cohorts.

### E. Calibration Logic

The numerical coefficients in the generative equations (e.g., the +0.3 on C in the Security equation, the −0.3 on T in the Status equation) were set by a two-step procedure. First, signs were fixed by theoretical priors: a regime variable increases a dimension it is predicted to amplify (e.g., E → Autonomy, Resilience) and decreases a dimension it is predicted to suppress (e.g., E → Security, Status). Second, magnitudes were calibrated by requiring that well-documented reference cohorts (U.S. Boomers, U.S. Generation Z, and the Mexican *Milagro* cohort) yield narrative vectors consistent with the secondary literature [4], [11], [12]. This calibration procedure is transparent but not yet empirical: Section V describes a validation instrument designed to replace these calibrations with data-driven estimates.

### F. The Formative Window

Following Mannheim [1], the regime variables are evaluated as averages over the period when the cohort was aged 10 to 25. For a cohort born between years *y*<sub>0</sub> and *y*<sub>1</sub>, the formative window is therefore (*y*<sub>0</sub> + 10, *y*<sub>1</sub> + 25). This operationalization is critical: it is the structural conditions of formation, not of birth or of current age, that determine the vector.

## IV. Application to the United States

### A. Historical Context

The U.S. case is archetypal because its twentieth century combined rare economic abundance with sharp episodes of institutional rupture. Four structural inflection points shape the regime trajectory. First, the post-World War II boom and the GI Bill [4] drove E to historic lows while C peaked near 77% in 1964 [5]. Second, the sequence of the Kennedy assassination (1963), the King and Kennedy assassinations (1968), and Watergate (1973) collapsed C from the low-70s to 36% in 1974 and 25% by 1980 [5]. Third, the emergence of personal computing in the 1980s and the public internet in the 1990s pushed T from near zero to historic highs [8]. Fourth, the compound shocks of 9/11 (2001), the 2008 financial crisis, and the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 produced a simultaneous collapse of C, rise of E (for younger cohorts), and saturation of I.

### B. Regime Variables Across Formative Windows

Table I summarizes the regime variables estimated for each U.S. generation's formative window.

**Table I. Regime variables by U.S. generation**

| Generation | Birth years | Formative window | E | T | C | V | I |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Baby Boomers | 1946–1964 | 1956–1989 | 2 | 2 | 8 | 3 | 2 |
| Generation X | 1965–1980 | 1975–2005 | 3 | 5 | 5 | 5 | 5 |
| Millennials | 1981–1996 | 1991–2021 | 5 | 8 | 3 | 4 | 8 |
| Generation Z | 1997–2012 | 2007–2037* | 5 | 10 | 2 | 6 | 10 |

*Generation Z's formative window is still ongoing; values reflect conditions through 2025.

**Sources:** Pew Research Center trust series [5]; FBI Uniform Crime Report and CDC WISQARS [6]; Federal Reserve Student Loan Debt Statistics [7]; OECD internet penetration data [8]; UNICEF child well-being indicators [9]; World Values Survey [10].

Institutional trust in the U.S. federal government, as tracked by Pew, moves from 73% in 1958 and 77% in 1964 to 36% after Watergate (1974), 25% in 1980, a transient 55% surge after 9/11 (2001), and a durable floor between 19% and 24% from 2007 onward [5]. This trajectory is central to the U.S. case: no other major developed economy in the twentieth century registered so steep a decline in the credibility of national institutions from so high a baseline. The consequence for the vector model is that C decreases by six points (from 8 to 2) across the four formative windows, while in most comparator countries the range of C is two or three points.

Student debt rose from $0.48 trillion in 2005 to approximately $1.73 trillion in 2025 [7], a fourfold increase in two decades. This is the principal mechanism by which economic scarcity (E) rose for Millennials and Generation Z despite aggregate GDP growth: the distribution of economic risk shifted from institutions (pensions, stable employment) to individuals (debt, gig work, deferred homeownership). The model's representation of E should therefore be understood not as aggregate scarcity but as *experienced scarcity during formation*.

Internet penetration moved from roughly 1% in 1990 to 10% in 1995, 44% in 2000, 74% in 2010, 91% in 2020, and 97% in 2024 [8]. The first derivative of this curve peaks in the late 1990s, making the Millennial formative window the period of maximal technological velocity. Generation Z, in contrast, is formed within a saturated regime: the rate of change is slower, but the baseline of information and technology is maximal. This distinction, which a single "T" variable cannot fully capture, motivates the separation of T (disruption) from I (saturation) in the model.

Homicide rates peaked at approximately 10 per 100,000 in 1980 and declined to 4.3 per 100,000 by 2025, with a COVID-era spike to 6.4 in 2020 [6]. However, homicide alone understates structural violence in the U.S. context. Mass shootings, which became culturally salient beginning with the Columbine event in 1999, function as background noise for Generation Z in particular and justify the V = 6 estimate even as the aggregate homicide rate declined. The opioid crisis, which produced over 100,000 overdose deaths annually by the early 2020s, is a further component of structural violence that does not appear in the standard homicide series.

### D. Interpretation

Four patterns emerge. First, **Security (S₁) and Status (S₂) decline monotonically** from Boomers to Generation Z, tracking the rise in economic scarcity (E) from 2 to 5 and the collapse in institutional trust (C) from 8 to 2. Second, **Identity (S₆) and Experience (S₅) rise monotonically**, driven by the T and I variables, which move from 2 to 10 over the same period. Third, **Autonomy (S₃) doubles** from Boomers (4) to Generation Z (8) as institutions become less credible guides for life choices. Fourth, **Resilience (S₈) rises sharply** from 3 to 8, reflecting the cumulative exposure of younger cohorts to compound crises.

The magnitude |**S**| is not monotonic: Generation X registers the lowest intensity (14.6), consistent with the characterization of that cohort as disengaged and self-contained [4], [11]. Generation Z produces the highest magnitude (16.2), indicating the most demanding life-meaning landscape.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Boomers** | 0.0 | 7.3 | 9.2 | 11.1 |
| **Gen X** | 7.3 | 0.0 | 6.3 | 5.9 |

Two observations are salient. First, the distance from Boomers to Generation Z (11.1) is approximately 50% greater than any adjacent-cohort distance, indicating cumulative rather than incremental cultural drift. Second, the Gen X–Gen Z distance (5.9) is *smaller* than the Boomer–Gen X distance (7.3), reflecting the fact that the transition out of the post-war trust regime was the single largest rupture in the series.

A third observation concerns asymmetry along the dimensions. Decomposing the Boomer–Gen Z distance by dimension shows that the largest contributions come from Identity (ΔS₆ = 5.0), Autonomy (ΔS₃ = 4.0), Resilience (ΔS₈ = 5.0), and Status (ΔS₂ = −6.0), while Experience and Affiliation contribute more modestly. This decomposition maps cleanly onto the regime variables: the Identity and Experience shifts track T and I; the Resilience shift tracks E and V; the Status and Security shifts track E and C. The model therefore yields an *accountable* distance—each unit of inter-generational separation is attributable to specific regime changes—rather than an opaque aggregate.

A fourth observation is that the distance matrix is not a time-stationary random walk. If regime variables evolved as a smooth trend, inter-cohort distances would grow linearly with time separation. The observed matrix is instead shaped by the specific timing of regime discontinuities: the trust collapse of 1963–1974, the technological acceleration of 1995–2010, and the compound shocks of 2001–2020. Adjacent cohorts that straddle such discontinuities are more distant than adjacent cohorts that do not.

### F. Detailed Narrative Interpretations

**Baby Boomers (1946–1964; formative window 1956–1989).** The Boomer regime combined historically low economic scarcity with historically high institutional trust. The GI Bill of 1944 produced the largest expansion of middle-class education and homeownership in U.S. history [4]; the suburban boom of Levittown and its imitators, the rise of network television as a unifying cultural medium, the Apollo program, and a pre-Watergate trust environment together generated a formative context in which individuals could plausibly expect institutions to deliver. The resulting vector (8, 8, 4, 7, 5, 4, 7, 3) exhibits the highest Security and Status in the series and a high Affiliation (7) consistent with Putnam's documentation of robust social capital prior to 1970 [12]. Transcendence scores at 7 reflect the coincidence of the civil-rights and counterculture movements, which mobilized large-scale moral projects. Resilience at 3 is the lowest in the series: this is the only U.S. generation formed under conditions that did not require pervasive adaptation.

**Generation X (1965–1980; formative window 1975–2005).** Generation X is the transitional cohort. Formed across stagflation, the post-Watergate trust collapse, the Reagan neoliberal turn, and the first wave of personal computing, it experienced partial regime shifts on every axis. Autonomy (8) is the dominant feature: latchkey childhoods, the normalization of divorce, and the retreat of institutions all produced a cohort whose narrative is "figure it out yourself." The low Transcendence (4) and low Status (5) distinguish this generation sharply from the Boomers: public meaning and institutional hierarchy had both lost credibility. Affiliation (4) is likewise depressed, consistent with Putnam's documentation of declining civic engagement through the 1980s and 1990s [12]. Generation X's vector magnitude (14.6) is the lowest in the series—an intensity of life-meaning demands muted by the cohort's ironic distance from grand narratives.

**Millennials (1981–1996; formative window 1991–2021).** The Millennial formative window brackets the most consequential media transition in history: from pre-internet childhood to smartphone adulthood. It also brackets 9/11 in adolescence or young adulthood, the 2008 financial crisis as a labor-market entry event, and the normalization of student debt above one trillion dollars [7]. The resulting vector (5, 3, 6, 7, 7, 8, 8, 5) is distinctive: Status is the lowest in the series (3), reflecting a cohort for whom conventional achievement markers became both delayed and devalued; Transcendence rises to 8, consistent with a purpose-driven economy and the widespread rhetoric of "meaning" and "impact"; Identity (8) reaches near-saturation, consistent with the psychological research on social-media-era self-construction [11]. Affiliation at 7 is notable: despite declining institutional trust, affiliative behavior (online communities, chosen families) remained high, suggesting that C's effect on S₄ is partly compensated by other channels of belonging.

**Generation Z (1997–2012; formative window 2007–2037).** Generation Z's formative window is still in progress, but the regime conditions through 2025 are sufficient to produce a stable estimate. The defining features are maximum technological disruption (T = 10) and informational saturation (I = 10), together with the lowest institutional trust (C = 2) in the series and elevated structural violence (V = 6) driven by mass shootings and the opioid crisis. The resulting vector (4, 2, 8, 5, 7, 9, 5, 8) is the most extreme in the series on four dimensions: lowest Status (2), highest Identity (9), highest Resilience (8), and joint-highest Autonomy (8). The narrative "nothing is guaranteed; I adapt" is a direct translation of this vector signature. Magnitude at 16.2 is the highest in the series, reflecting a life-meaning landscape of maximally demanding inputs and maximally diverse priorities.

### G. Cross-National Contrast: The Mexican Case

Applying the same model to Mexico produces vectors for four cohorts commonly labeled *Milagro* (roughly 1946–1964, formed during the *Milagro Mexicano*), *Crisis* (1965–1980, formed during the 1982 debt crisis and the 1994 Tequila Crisis), *Transición* (1981–1996, formed during democratization and internet expansion), and *Saturación* (1997–2012). Four comparative findings are notable:

1. **U.S. Boomers and Mexican *Milagro* exhibit similar magnitudes and profiles**, both formed under functional institutional regimes with low violence, though U.S. C is higher than Mexican C.
2. **U.S. Generation X faced lower E than Mexican *Crisis***, whose formative window included the 1982 and 1994 financial collapses; however, their autonomy and resilience scores converge.
3. **U.S. Millennials and Mexican *Transición* are the most similar pair in the comparison**, both formed during internet saturation and institutional disillusionment. This cross-national convergence on identity (S₆) and experience (S₅) is the strongest evidence in the dataset for a globalized formative regime.
4. **U.S. Generation Z differs from Mexican *Saturación* primarily on resilience**: Mexican *Saturación* registers V near 9 due to narco-related violence, pushing S₈ to 9, while U.S. Generation Z registers V near 6, yielding S₈ = 8. Both cohorts reach maximum I, confirming that informational saturation is now a global regime variable.

These results illustrate the core methodological point: generational labels imported from the United States are not interchangeable with cohort labels constructed for other national regimes, and the vector model renders the divergence explicit.

## V. Preliminary Validation

A web-based self-assessment instrument is under development to validate the model empirically. Respondents complete a 40-item questionnaire (five items per dimension) that yields an individual vector **S**<sup>(i)</sup>. Items are drawn from validated scales where available (Schwartz Value Survey subscales, Connor–Davidson Resilience Scale, Meaning in Life Questionnaire) and adapted for brevity and cross-cultural portability. Responses on a seven-point Likert scale are rescaled to the 0–10 interval to match the model's output space.

Three questions guide validation. First, do individuals whose formative window coincides with a given cohort cluster near the predicted cohort vector, and is the within-cluster dispersion smaller than the between-cluster distance matrix of Table IV? Second, does the model's predicted inter-generational distance pattern—in particular, the large Boomer–Gen X jump and the smaller Millennial–Gen Z gap—replicate in the respondent data? Third, do *migrants*, operationalized as individuals whose formative window was spent in a country different from their current residence, cluster with the regime of their formative window rather than that of their residence? A positive answer to the third question would provide the strongest evidence that regime-under-formation, rather than current environment or birth year alone, drives life-meaning structure.

A complementary validation strategy applies the model to cohorts for which detailed survey data already exist. The World Values Survey [10] item banks can be mapped onto the eight dimensions, yielding empirical vectors for over eighty countries and several waves. Preliminary work suggests that the model's predicted ordering of magnitudes across cohorts is reproduced in these data; full results will be reported in a forthcoming companion paper together with sensitivity analyses on the coefficient choices of Section III-C.

## VI. Discussion

### A. Theoretical Implications

The vector model provides a principled alternative to narrative generational labels. It makes explicit what such labels obscure: that the observed differences between cohorts reflect differences in regime variables, not differences in birth year per se. When two cohorts in different countries share regime conditions, the model predicts—correctly, in the case of U.S. Millennials and Mexican *Transición*—that their life-meaning profiles converge. When two cohorts share a birth year but differ in regime conditions, the model predicts divergence. This reframes the debate: the unit of analysis is not the generation but the *regime under formation*.

The model also sheds light on why Strauss–Howe cyclical theories [4] appear to work within the United States and fail elsewhere. The cyclical pattern is an artifact of the specific U.S. regime trajectory—post-war boom → trust collapse → technological surge → compound crisis—not a general law. Other national trajectories produce different cycles or no cycles at all. A country that never experienced a post-war prosperity boom will not produce a Boomer-type cohort, regardless of the birth years involved. A country whose institutional trust remained stable through the 1970s will not produce a Gen-X-type cohort. The vector model formalizes this observation.

A further theoretical implication concerns the relationship between individual and cohort vectors. The model predicts cohort-level vectors from cohort-level regime variables, but individuals within a cohort will differ in the intensity with which they experienced those regimes. A Gen-Z respondent raised in a low-trust, high-violence urban environment will exhibit regime exposures closer to the cohort mean (or more extreme), while one raised in a high-trust rural environment will exhibit regime exposures closer to those of earlier cohorts. The vector model therefore predicts that within-cohort variance in life-meaning vectors should be structured by within-cohort variance in regime exposure—a testable claim.

### B. Practical Implications

Three practical applications follow. First, in public policy, interventions targeted at cohorts should be calibrated to regime variables rather than birth cohorts. A policy that assumes, on the basis of a U.S.-derived Millennial label, that individuals born in 1985 in any country share a common life-meaning profile will misfire where regime conditions diverge. A policy calibrated to regime variables can be applied across contexts with appropriate adjustment.

Second, in labor markets and organizational behavior, inter-generational friction is better understood as friction between formative regimes rather than as friction between ages. A Boomer manager and a Gen-Z employee do not conflict because of age; they conflict because they formed under regimes that assigned different priorities to Security, Status, Autonomy, Affiliation, Experience, Identity, Transcendence, and Resilience. Framing the conflict in regime terms makes it negotiable; framing it in generational terms makes it essentialized.

Third, in comparative research, the model provides a common metric for cross-national cohort comparison, avoiding the category error of applying U.S. generational labels to non-U.S. populations. Researchers can compute vectors for any cohort in any country from publicly available regime data and evaluate hypotheses about cross-national convergence or divergence without relying on narrative equivalence between, for example, "Millennials" in the United States and "Millennials" in Japan, Mexico, or South Africa.

### C. Limitations

Four limitations merit explicit acknowledgment. First, the regime variables are themselves composite indices whose operationalization involves choices—of weighting, of temporal smoothing, and of data source—that deserve sensitivity analysis. An index of economic scarcity constructed from real wages and unemployment will not in general coincide with one constructed from household debt and consumption volatility; the present paper reports a single calibration but future work should evaluate the stability of the vector outputs under alternative indexations.

Second, the coefficients in the generative equations were calibrated qualitatively against narrative descriptions in the secondary literature and require empirical refinement through the validation instrument of Section V. A full data-driven calibration would estimate coefficients by minimizing the discrepancy between predicted and observed vectors across a reference set of cohorts; this is a natural next step.

Third, the model treats cohorts as homogeneous, ignoring intra-cohort heterogeneity by class, race, gender, and region. A richer version of the model would allow the regime variables themselves to vary within a cohort—for instance, urban-rural differences in C, or class-based differences in E—producing a distribution of vectors rather than a single representative point. The present paper is best read as a first-order approximation to which such second-order refinements can be added.

Fourth, the formative window is treated as a rectangular function over ages 10–25, which implicitly assigns equal developmental weight to each year in that range. A weighted window—for example, a Gaussian centered on age 16 with a standard deviation of four years—is likely to be more psychologically accurate and is straightforwardly implemented. Erikson's developmental framework [14] suggests that the weight profile should peak in mid-adolescence, when identity consolidation is most intense.

Fifth, and more fundamentally, the eight dimensions themselves are a modeling choice. Alternative decompositions—five dimensions, ten dimensions, or a hierarchical structure with second-order factors—are entirely possible and would produce different, though related, vector signatures. The justification for the present choice is parsimony together with coverage of the principal value families documented in the comparative literature [2], [3], [10], [13].

### D. Implications for the Category "Generation"

A final implication concerns the conceptual status of the term *generation* itself. Under the vector model, a generation is not a fundamental unit but an emergent clustering: it is the observation that individuals whose formative windows overlap significantly tend to produce similar vectors, and that abrupt transitions in regime variables produce discontinuities in the sequence of vectors that make cohort labels informationally useful. When regime variables change slowly, generations are not well-defined; when they change abruptly, generations emerge naturally at the discontinuities. This perspective explains why U.S. generational labels are sharpest around the transitions of the 1940s, the late 1960s, and the early 2000s: these are the periods at which the regime variables changed most rapidly. It also explains why labels imported into contexts with different regime trajectories are informationally poor: the discontinuities do not line up.

---

## VII. Conclusion

This paper has proposed a multidimensional vector model of life meaning and applied it to the four canonical U.S. generations. The model represents each cohort as an eight-dimensional vector computed from five regime variables evaluated over a Mannheimian formative window. Results for the United States show that life-meaning magnitude has increased from 14.6 (Generation X) to 16.2 (Generation Z), with the principal drivers being the collapse of institutional trust (C) and the surge in technological disruption (T) and informational saturation (I). The distance matrix confirms that cumulative drift from Boomers to Generation Z substantially exceeds any adjacent-cohort rupture, and that the largest single rupture occurred between Boomers and Generation X. A comparison with Mexico shows that U.S. Millennials and Mexican *Transición* exhibit remarkable convergence, confirming the emergence of a globalized formative regime, while U.S. Generation Z and Mexican *Saturación* diverge primarily on resilience driven by country-specific violence regimes. The model thereby offers a portable, generative, and geometric alternative to narrative generational labels, and provides a testable framework for cohort analysis across national contexts.
